## Research

Most population genetics is done one species at a time. We try to do it for whole communities. The questions are old ones: how did the species that live together in a place get there, how did they respond to past climate change, and what does that tell us about what comes next. To get at them we build models of demography, divergence, gene flow, and community assembly, and fit them to genomic data from many co-distributed species at once, mostly by simulation-based inference (approximate Bayesian computation, and lately machine learning). We also work on single marine systems when the natural history is too good to pass up.

Some of what we are working on now:

- A hybrid zone between the sea stars *Asterias rubens* and *A. forbesi* on the Maine coast, using whole genomes to work out when and how the two species started mixing.
- Gene flow and divergence between sympatric lineages of the sea anemone *Phymactis papillosa* along the Pacific coast, from Mexico to Chile.
- Whole-genome population genomics of periwinkles (*Littorina*) and rockweed (*Ascophyllum nodosum*) across the wave-exposure gradient of the Maine intertidal.
- Genome-environment associations across 27 co-distributed taxa in the Brazilian Atlantic Forest, with Ana Carnaval's lab ([paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/mec.70545)).
- MESS, the Massive Eco-Evolutionary Synthesis Simulator, a joint model of species abundances, genetic diversity, and trait diversity for whole communities.
